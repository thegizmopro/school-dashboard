Our School Budget, in Plain Language

A parent-friendly summary of the Harmony Union School District 2025-26 adopted budget
Read this first: this page summarizes the 2025-26 budget, adopted June 2025. That school year has since ended, and several open questions from it (the architect lawsuit, the busing decision, planned layoffs) have since been resolved. For the current picture, see the newer budget or ask at a board meeting — every board meeting date is on the main dashboard calendar.

What the district is

Harmony Union School District runs Harmony Elementary (TK–1st) and Salmon Creek Charter (2nd–8th) on one shared campus in Occidental — about 225 students total. The official budget document is 72 pages of state-required forms; this page covers what parents most need to know.

1. Savings fell sharply in three years

Flexible savings dropped from about $3.3M in 2022-23 to about $680K entering 2025-26. The district spent more than it took in three years running ($750K–$960K/year). The main driver: the Measure C campus modernization went well over budget, and about $1.1M of general operating money finished the job.

2. A balanced year — if several things go right

The 2025-26 plan projected a small surplus (~$291K), mostly from one-time fund transfers, and was certified "positive." But the cushion above the state-required 5% reserve shrinks fast: about $187K (2025-26), $61K (2026-27), $5K (2027-28) — after which small deficits return.

3. Lawsuit against the architects

The district sued the bond project's architects, expecting a settlement of roughly $650K–$750K during 2025-26 (50–60% of the claim), earmarked for the emergency savings account. Whether it settled — and for how much — was the single biggest open question in that budget.

4. Staffing

  • Two teaching positions were slated for cuts in 2026-27 (funded only temporarily in 2025-26); teacher salary costs drop ~12%.
  • Settled contracts: 3% raises each year.
  • Health premiums rose ~8.6–8.8%.
  • Admin: superintendent/principal, assistant principal, chief business official, assistant business official, office manager — all full time.

5. Attendance is money

State funding follows average daily attendance, not enrollment — roughly $14,500 per student, per year. Every absence costs the school real money. Past years saw attendance overestimated and state checks missed; enrollment was projected flat to slightly down by 2027-28.

6. Other pressures

  • Special education: costs ~$1.25M; the general budget covers ~$958K of it.
  • Federal: ~$174K less after losing the school-improvement (CSI) grant.
  • Busing: home-to-school transport budgeted ~$343K; costs rose sharply after the county bus agency added a large district. The board was deciding in late 2025 whether to leave the agency (it subsequently did).

7. Bright spots

  • After-school/summer (ELOP) funding up $100K.
  • After-school fee revenue expected to grow.
  • Transitional Kindergarten funding expanding.
  • Kitchen upgrade grants received.

8. The bond is separate

The Measure C bond (~$9.4M remaining, 27 years) is repaid by property taxpayers through a separate fund — it does not come out of the classroom budget.

Good questions to ask the board

  1. Did the architect lawsuit settle, and for how much?
  2. What's the plan to rebuild reserves, with the cushion projected near zero by 2027-28?
  3. How did the teaching-position cuts affect class staffing?
  4. What changed in the newest budget versus this one?

Agenda requests are due to the district by 3:00 PM on the Thursday before each board meeting.
